Reading the Completions tab

How HEIDI measures program-mix similarity

The Program Fit and Similar Institutions views score how closely two institutions' degree programs match. This page explains the score in plain terms, shows what it does and does not measure, and gives a calibrated guide to reading it.

The core idea

Every institution graduates students from a particular mix of programs. One college might award 40% of its degrees in business and 30% in nursing; another might award half of its degrees in engineering. HEIDI describes each institution by that mix: the share of its degree completions in each program, using the federal CIP taxonomy at the 4-digit level (which groups related majors, so all registered nursing degrees count as one program).

The similarity score compares two institutions' mixes and returns a single number between 0 and 1. A score of 1 means the two award degrees in exactly the same proportions. A score of 0 means they have no programs in common. In between, the score rises as the mixes overlap more.

The score measures the shape of the mix, not its size. A university graduating 5,000 students a year and a college graduating 200 can score 1.0 against each other if their degrees are spread across programs in the same proportions. Size, selectivity, quality and location are not part of the score; the dashboard's other tabs cover those.

Picture each college's shares as an arrow: one dimension per program, and the arrow points further along a dimension the larger that program's share. Two colleges with the same proportions point the same way. The score is the cosine of the angle between the two arrows: 1 when they point the same way, 0 when they are at right angles (no programs in common).

Two institutions' program mixes drawn as arrows in a simplified two-program space. The angle between them sets the score.

The arithmetic

similarity(A, B) = (A · B) / (‖A‖ × ‖B‖)
A · B adds up, program by program, the product of the two shares. ‖A‖ is the square root of the sum of A's squared shares.
Products added up: (0.40 × 0.35) + (0.30 × 0.25) + (0.20 × 0.25) + (0.10 × 0.15) + (0 × 0) = 0.280
‖A‖: √(0.40² + 0.30² + 0.20² + 0.10²) = √0.300 = 0.548
‖B‖: √(0.35² + 0.25² + 0.25² + 0.15²) = √0.270 = 0.520
Score: 0.280 / (0.548 × 0.520) = 0.98

A score of 0.98 says the two colleges' mixes are nearly the same: the small differences in proportions barely move the angle. Shares are never negative, so the score always falls between 0 and 1.

Reading the score

Real institutions rarely score as high as the two colleges above, because most pairs have little in common. HEIDI scored every pair of the 1,588 private not-for-profit four-year institutions in the United States with degree completions in the 2023-24 to 2025-26 IPEDS collections (1,260,078 pairs): the typical pair scores about 0.04; only about 1 pair in 6 reaches 0.40, 1 in 14 reaches 0.60, and 1 in 50 reaches 0.80. Because the score is dominated by the largest programs, an institution with one very large program (nursing or business, say) scores highly against any other institution where that program is also large, whatever else they teach. That is a feature when the question is "who graduates students in the same fields in the same proportions", and a limitation when the question is "who offers the same range of programs". HEIDI reports a second number, coverage, for the second question.

Try giving one institution a large program the other lacks: the score falls quickly, because the arrows swing apart along that dimension. Then try trimming both institutions' smaller programs to zero: the score hardly moves, because small programs contribute little to the angle.

Why this score and not a simpler one

The obvious alternative is to subtract one institution's shares from the other's and average the gaps. That treats a difference of five points in a 40% program the same as five points in a 5% program, and it depends on how many programs an institution spreads its degrees across. The cosine score weighs each program by how large it is at both institutions, ignores the roughly 1,500 programs that neither offers (two colleges earn no credit for both lacking petroleum engineering), and is unaffected by how many graduates either institution has. Those three properties are what make it usable across institutions of very different sizes.

How HEIDI uses it

HEIDI builds each institution's program mix from the three most recent IPEDS Completions collections: first-major degree completions at the 4-digit CIP level, certificates excluded, added across the three years before shares are taken. It then uses the score in two views of the Completions tab:

  • Program Fit. For each comparison group on the dashboard, the median similarity of its members to the focal institution, the share of members scoring 0.60 or more, and the three most similar members. Beside it sits coverage: the share of the focal institution's degree completions that fall in programs a member also graduates students in, which answers the range-of-programs question the score does not.
  • Most Similar Members and Similar Institutions. Each comparison group's members ranked by similarity to the focal institution, and a national list limited to institutions of the same control and level and between half and twice the focal institution's total FTE, with the Carnegie class, size, finances and group membership beside each score so the reader can see why an institution is or is not in a comparison group.

What the completions data can and cannot say

  • Completions describe graduates, not enrollment or offerings. Each IPEDS collection reports awards conferred in the academic year before its label, and the three-year window reaches further back still.
  • A program launched recently appears in these views only once it has graduates, typically two to four years after launch. The Inventory view of the Completions tab lists programs reported to IPEDS with no graduates yet, which is the earliest the data can show a new program.
  • A program that has closed keeps appearing until its last students finish.
  • Grouping at the 4-digit CIP level means that two institutions can score as closely matched while offering different specialties within the same families.
